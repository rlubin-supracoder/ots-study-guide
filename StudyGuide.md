## 01 – Professional/Unprofessional Relationships

### Describe professional and unprofessional relationships.

- **Professional Relationships**
  - Reflect Air Force and Space Force standards of conduct and core values.
  - Enhance morale, unit cohesion, good order, discipline, and mission focus.
  - Preserve respect for authority.
- **Unprofessional Relationships (UPR)**
  - Detract from the authority of superiors.
  - Result in, or appear to show, favoritism, partiality, or misuse of position.
  - Abandon organizational goals for personal interests.
  - Can exist between officers, enlisted, military and civilian/contractor personnel.

### Summarize the policy on professional and unprofessional relationships.

- **Official Concern:** Personal relationships become a concern when they adversely affect morale, good order, discipline, respect for authority, unit cohesion, or the mission.
- **Prohibited When:** They compromise the chain of command, create perceptions of favoritism, involve improper use of grade for personal gain, are exploitative, or negatively impact the mission.
- **Responsibility:** All members are accountable, but the senior member in a relationship is primarily responsible for maintaining professional standards.
- **Scope:** Policy applies on or off-duty, across all branches and components, and includes interactions on social media.

### Summarize the general and specific prohibitions relating to unprofessional relationships for officers.

- **General Prohibitions**
  - Officers must avoid activities with enlisted members that could prejudice good order and discipline, discredit the armed forces, or compromise the officer's standing.
- **Specific Prohibitions — Officers will not:**
  - Gamble with enlisted members.
  - Lend money to or borrow money from enlisted members.
  - Share living accommodations with enlisted members.
  - Engage in business or solicit sales with enlisted members.
  - Date or engage in sexual relations with enlisted members.
- **Fraternization**
  - A UPR between an officer and an enlisted member that violates customs of acceptable behavior.
  - Prejudices good order and discipline or discredits the armed services.
  - A subsequent marriage does not excuse prior acts of fraternization.

### Summarize the effects of unprofessional relationships on unit cohesiveness.

- **Erodes Trust & Respect:** Undermines a leader's integrity and creates doubt in their motives.
- **Degrades Morale:** Creates perceptions of favoritism and an "in crowd," which causes morale and discipline problems.
- **Undermines Authority:** Weakens the leadership positions of other leaders in the unit who are trying to maintain standards.
- **Impacts Mission:** Can lead to significant degradation in unit cohesion, respect for authority, discipline, and ultimately, mission accomplishment. Sexual relationships have an increased potential to cause significant degradation.

## 02 – Core Values

### Explain how the virtues relate to the USAF and USSF Core Values.

**EXPLAIN: Virtues & Core Values Relationship**

- **Virtues**
  - Desired behaviors and characteristics
  - Qualities considered morally good or desirable

- **Relationship**
  - Virtues demonstrate that one truly values Integrity, Service, and Excellence

## 03 – US Constitution

### Recall the branch of government that establishes military authority.

- Executive Branch
- Derived from Article II of the Constitution
- President as Commander in Chief

### List the three provisions embodied in the US Constitution.

- **Enumerated Powers:** Limits federal government to powers explicitly granted in the Constitution.
- **Separation of Powers:** Divides government into three independent branches (Legislative, Executive, Judicial) to prevent concentration of power.
- **Federalism:** Divides and shares power between the national and state governments.

### Recall the Amendments to the US Constitution that govern military officer authority.
- **Bill of Rights**
- 1st: restricts officers from considering the following when making decisions: race, gender, religion, race, and politics.
- 2nd: prohibits taking weapons from the public (state militias).
- 3rd: prohibits the military from forcing the public to house them (quarters).
- 4th: prohibits military from searching without probable cause (exception: commanders can search areas within their control)
- 5th: prevents questioning without due process, double jeopardy, and self-incrimination 
- 6th, 7th, 8th: prohibits punishment without trial (exception: Article 15)
-	6th and 7th: juries
-	8th: bail
- 10th: powers not delegated to the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the states, are reserved to the states/people (the “catch-all”)

## 04 – Full Range Leadership

### Differentiate the characteristics of laissez-faire, transactional, and transformational leadership.

- **Laissez-Faire (The "Hands-Off" Leader)**
  - Absence of action or leadership; avoids making decisions.
  - Disconnected from the mission and fails to follow up.
  - Does not develop subordinates.
  - An approach of non-leadership that should be avoided.
- **Transactional Leadership (The "Task-Focused" Leader)**
  - Focuses on exchanges between leaders and followers.
  - Seeks to maintain stability and achieve goals through a system of rewards and corrective actions.
  - Includes three behaviors:
    - **Contingent Reward (CR):** Sets expectations and provides rewards when they are met.
    - **Management by Exception-Active (MBE-A):** Constantly monitors for deviations from standards and corrects them immediately.
    - **Management by Exception-Passive (MBE-P):** Waits for problems to arise before intervening.
- **Transformational Leadership (The "People-Focused" Leader)**
  - Involves creating personal relationships to motivate and inspire followers.
  - Attentive to followers' needs, helps them reach their fullest potential.
  - Includes four behaviors:
    - **Idealized Influence (II):** Acts as a positive role model; "walking the talk."
    - **Inspirational Motivation (IM):** Inspires and motivates with a shared vision; "talking the talk."
    - **Intellectual Stimulation (IS):** Stimulates creative thinking and challenges old ways of doing things.
    - **Individual Consideration (IC):** Acts as a coach or mentor, focusing on subordinate development.

### Describe how to apply Full-Range Leadership.

- **Not a "Bad" to "Good" Continuum**
  - The model represents a range of leadership activity and effectiveness.
  - Different circumstances require different approaches.
- **Blend Approaches**
  - Effective leaders utilize several approaches simultaneously.
  - Blending and emphasizing approaches based on the situation is critical.
- **Mission Accomplishment**
  - Particularly helpful in the military to resolve issues in dynamic environments.
  - Illustrates the behaviors needed to accomplish the mission.
- **Avoid Laissez-Faire**
  - This style is the absence of leadership and is counterproductive.

## 05 – Followership

### Five Followership Styles

- **Sheep:** Passive and not a critical thinker. They wait for instructions, do only what's asked, and have no initiative.
- **Yes People:** Active, but not a critical thinker. They depend on the leader for direction and won't question any orders.
- **Survivors:** A mix of all styles. They do just enough to get by and avoid making waves or taking risks.
- **Alienated Followers:** A critical thinker, but passive. They are often cynical and criticize leadership but don't offer solutions.
- **Effective Followers:** Active and a critical thinker. They are proactive, take initiative, work well with others, and aren't afraid to raise concerns with their leader.

### Characteristics of an Effective Follower

- Support your boss; don't undermine them.
- If you disagree with the boss, do it privately.
- Take initiative to make decisions and take action.
- Accept responsibility when it's offered.
- Tell the truth.
- Do your homework and anticipate your boss's questions.
- Know your own strengths and weaknesses.
- Keep your boss informed.
- Fix problems you find without worrying about who gets the credit.
- Don't forget your family's needs.

### Followership & Leadership Connection

- To be a good leader, you must first be a good follower.
- Most people are leaders and followers at the same time.
- Developing good followers is the first step to creating good leaders.
- Good followers often transition into leadership roles.
- Effective leaders and effective followers share many of the same skills and qualities.

## 06 – Team Building

### Remember the four stages of team growth.

- **Forming:** Team members determine their roles and transition from individual to member status.
- **Storming:** Conflict arises as members resist collaboration and argue over team actions; this is the most difficult stage.
- **Norming:** Cohesiveness and commitment are established, conflict is reduced, and team norms are set.
- **Performing:** The team becomes proficient, solves problems, and members accept each other's strengths and weaknesses.

### Describe leader and follower actions that contribute to effective teams.

**Forming Stage ("Director")**

- *Leader Actions:*
  - Be directive.
  - Help the team get to know one another.
  - Clearly define roles and the team's mission/tasks.

**Storming Stage ("Coach")**

- *Leader Actions:*
  - Act as a coach to control chaos.
  - Create a climate of open discussion and listen to concerns.
  - Get input from members on how to achieve goals.
  - Be encouraging, supportive, and facilitate conflict resolution.
- *Follower Actions:*
  - Voice concerns and provide input on goals.

**Norming Stage ("Collaborator")**

- *Leader Actions:*
  - Act as a facilitator and collaborator.
  - Delegate responsibilities to build member confidence.
  - Maximize members' strengths and contributions.
- *Follower Actions:*
  - Establish cohesiveness and commitment.
  - Reconcile competing loyalties and increase cooperation.

**Performing Stage ("Visionary")**

- *Leader Actions:*
  - Act as a visionary, focusing on maintaining success.
  - Plan for the future and cultivate leadership talent within the team.
  - Foster new ideas and ensure solid mechanisms for feedback.
- *Follower Actions:*
  - Embrace roles and accept each other's strengths/weaknesses.
  - Begin solving problems and implementing change effectively.

## 07 – Problem Solving

### Define problem-solving.

Purposeful act of observing a problem, considering solutions, and deciding to solve it.

### Compare the 8-step Practical Problem-Solving Method to the OODA Loop Model.

**OODA Loop:**

- 4 steps (Observe, Orient, Decide, Act)
- Rapid decision-making
- Combat/time-compressed environments
- Single decision maker
- Continuous cycle

**PPSM (8-Step):**

- 8 detailed steps
- Long-term problem solving
- Team-centered approach
- Process improvement focus
- Systematic methodology

### Understand the steps for the 8-Step Practical Problem-Solving Method.

1. Clarify the Problem
2. Break Down Problem & ID Performance Gaps
3. Set Improvement Targets
4. Determine Root Causes
5. Develop Countermeasures
6. See Countermeasures Through
7. Confirm Results and Process
8. Standardizing Successful Processes

## 08 – Critical Thinking

### Summarize the definition of critical thinking.

*"The process of thinking carefully about a subject or idea, without allowing feelings or opinions to affect you."*

- Raises vital questions and problems
- Gathers and assesses relevant information
- Well-reasoned conclusions and solutions
- Open-minded

### Discuss methods for evaluating information.

Several methods for evaluating information involve distinguishing between different types of claims and information:

- **Statement vs. Argument:** A statement can be true or false. An argument provides a reason (or "premise") for a given conclusion.
- **Fact vs. Opinion:** A fact is something true that can be proven and is objective and verifiable. An opinion is a subjective belief a person has formed that cannot be verified.
- **Objective vs. Subjective Claim:** An objective claim is factually true or false, independent of what people think. A subjective claim's truth or falsity depends on what people think.

### Explain the nine intellectual standards used in assessing one's thinking.

Intellectual standards applied to thinking to verify the quality of information being presented or received. They are:

1. **Clarity:** A "gateway standard" that is relevant to all others. If a statement is unclear, you cannot determine its accuracy or relevance.
2. **Accuracy:** Addresses the validity and truthfulness of the information.
3. **Precision:** Helps refine information by adding necessary details.
4. **Relevance:** Evaluates what is most related to the issue at hand.
5. **Depth:** Addresses the complexities and deeper layers of an issue.
6. **Breadth:** Addresses various interpretations and other points of view.
7. **Logic:** Ensures that thoughts are mutually supporting and make sense in combination.
8. **Significance:** Identifies the most important pieces of an argument or issue.
9. **Fairness:** Acts as a "gut check" to avoid personal interests, ego, and unjustified assumptions.

### Explain cognitive domain operations and their relationship to disinformation.

- **Cognitive Domain Operations**
  - Aims to influence an adversary's will, beliefs, and thinking to affect their decisions and actions.
  - Targets the human brain as the main combat space.
  - Uses psychological weaknesses like fear, anxiety, and suspicion to weaken the will to fight.
  - Aims to make an opponent feel "I can't," "I dare not," and ultimately "I don't want to."
  - Happens constantly, in peace and war, across political, economic, and social fields.
  - Information is the primary "ammunition," and social media is a main battleground.
- **Relationship to Disinformation**
  - Disinformation is a key tool in cognitive operations.
  - Operations use cognitive interference, confusion, and information blocking to cause misjudgment.
  - Social media, AI, and deep fakes are used to spread disinformation and control narratives.
  - It exploits confirmation bias, where people favor information that confirms their existing beliefs, making them vulnerable to disinformation.
  - The goal is to control the "Three Powers": defining an event, dominating its process, and judging its outcome, often through spreading false or misleading information.

### Describe the elements of reasoning that are used in the critical thinking process.

- **Purpose:** The objective or goal of your thinking.
- **Question at Issue:** The central problem or question that needs to be answered.
- **Points of View:** The different perspectives from which to see the issue.
- **Information:** The data, facts, and evidence used to support reasoning.
- **Concepts:** The theories, principles, and ideas that frame the thinking.
- **Assumptions:** Beliefs and values that are taken for granted.
- **Inferences:** The conclusions drawn from information and assumptions.
- **Implications & Consequences:** The potential outcomes of a decision or action.

### Discuss common thinking errors that can lead to faulty thinking.

- **Relying on System 1 Thinking:** Using fast, instinctive, and emotional thinking instead of slow, deliberate, and logical System 2 thinking.
- **Asking the Wrong Questions:** Failing to identify the correct problem or asking questions in a way that doesn't lead to understanding.
- **Thinking Too Quickly:** Rushing to judgment without sufficient analysis.
- **Stereotypes:** Making snap judgments and oversimplifications that prohibit critical thought.
- **Cognitive Biases:**
  - **Halo Effect:** Allowing a single trait to define a whole person or situation.
  - **Belief Perseverance/Confirmation Bias:** Favoring information that confirms existing beliefs and perspectives.
  - **Deflection:** Answering the wrong question to steer a conversation away from its original intent.
  - **Activated Ignorance:** Actively using information that is false but mistakenly believed to be true.

## 09 – Establishing Expectations

### Describe the importance of establishing expectations.

- **Identify Goals:** To clarify objectives for the team.
- **Provide Roadmap:** Offers a clear path to achieve goals.
- **Link to Mission:** Connects individual duties to unit standards and the overall mission.
- **Reduce Stress:** Minimizes confusion and stress by providing clarity.
- **Foster Connection:** Helps personnel understand how their role supports the broader organizational mission.

### Summarize the guidelines for establishing expectations.

- **Understand Importance:** Know why the expectations are being set.
- **Know When & Where:** Recognize it's a continuous cycle: establish, observe performance, and adjust expectations as needed.
- **Communicate Clearly:** Ensure expectations are simple and easily understood; know what your own boss expects first.

- **Seek Buy-In:** Include the team in the process, set achievable goals, maintain a positive tone, and show trust to foster ownership.
- **Track Progress:** Plan how to monitor activities by outlining steps, setting milestones, tracking progress, and capturing results.

## 10 – Conflict Management

### Describe sources of conflict (factors).

- **Organizational Sources**
  - **Communication Factors:** Misinterpreted, inaccurate, or incomplete information leading to frustration.
  - **Structural Factors:** Issues arising from the size of an organization, such as information distortion.
  - **Rewards:** Conflict can arise if reward systems are perceived as unfair or biased.
  - **Resource Interdependence:** Conflict is likely when resources like time, people, or money are perceived as scarce.
- **Personal Behavior Factors**
  - **Values:** Conflict generated when personal values, such as those related to religion or politics, are questioned or criticized.
  - **Perception:** Different views on concepts like fairness or quality can cause conflict, especially when individuals assume everyone defines terms the same way.
  - **Personality:** Clashes can occur between uncomplimentary personalities, like a highly authoritarian person and someone with low self-esteem.
- **Additional Factors**
  - **Who you're dealing with:** The approach to conflict may differ with a commander versus a subordinate.
  - **What the stakes are:** The importance of the issue to each party determines their level of assertiveness and cooperation.
  - **What the situation is:** Time-sensitive situations, like on a battlefield, may limit the available management styles.

### Compare the five conflict management styles and problem-solving approaches.

The five conflict management styles from the Thomas-Kilmann model align closely with the five problem-solving approaches from the Air Force Negotiation Center (AFNC). Both models use two primary variables: one relating to assertiveness or task orientation, and the other to cooperativeness or people orientation.

| Thomas-Kilmann | AFNC | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Competing/Forcing | Insist | High assertiveness/task focus, low cooperation/people focus. Aims to overwhelm an opponent with authority or power. Useful when the objective is paramount, regardless of the relationship. |
| Avoiding | Evade | Low assertiveness/task focus, low cooperation/people focus. A passive approach where the issue is often ignored or delayed. Appropriate when the issue is unimportant or more information is needed. |
| Accommodating | Comply | Low assertiveness/task focus, high cooperation/people focus. Involves giving in to the other person's wishes to preserve the relationship. Useful when the relationship is more important than the task. |
| Compromising | Settle | Moderate assertiveness/task focus, moderate cooperation/people focus. Seeks a solution that partially satisfies both parties through mutual concessions. A quick solution when parties are willing to give something up. |
| Collaborating | Cooperate | High assertiveness/task focus, high cooperation/people focus. Aims to find a "win-win" solution that fully satisfies the concerns of both sides. Requires trust, openness, and shared information to create the best outcome. |

## 10 – Change Management

### Recognize Lewin's Change Management Model.

**The Model**

- Developed in the 1940s by Kurt Lewin.
- Three stages: **Unfreezing → Changing → Refreezing**.
- Each stage has its own opportunities and difficulties.
- Two competing forces act on every change effort:
  - **Driving Forces:** Push in a particular direction to produce change.
  - **Restraining Forces:** Act to restrain or decrease the driving forces and inhibit change.
- Dr. John Kotter's 8-Step Change model is used to further develop the Unfreezing, Changing, and Refreezing process.

**Stage 1 — Unfreezing**

- Deliberate management activity to prepare people, processes, machines, etc. for change.
- Requires leaders to create awareness and understanding of the change.
  - Communication is key to succeed.
  - Members must know the "why" and "how" of the change.
- End goal is to inspire a personal desire in members to change.
  - Buy-in and collaboration.
- **Positive Leader Actions (Kotter steps 1–4):**
  1. Create Urgency
  2. Form a Powerful Coalition
  3. Create a Vision
  4. Communicate the Vision

**Stage 2 — Changing**

- Leaders begin to enact the proposed vision and change plan.
- Change consists of changes to technology, tasks, structures, personnel, mission, and more.
- All change MUST be monitored.
- Leaders should address any concerns or resistance immediately.
- **Positive Leader Actions (Kotter steps 5–6):**

  5. **Remove Obstacles (empowerment)**
     - Use the coalition to assist with necessary policy changes to support the change.
     - Instill knowledge.
     - Develop ability.
  6. **Create Short-Term Wins**
     - "Quick-wins" to reach milestones and reduce criticism.
     - Recognize strong performers.

**Stage 3 — Refreezing**

- Even with the previous two stages being successful, change efforts can still be derailed before refreezing occurs.
- Stay engaged and continually evaluate results:
  - Is change achieving desired results?
  - Does the change require additional support or resources?
- Formalize processes.
- Ensure stability in the long term.
- **Positive Leader Actions (Kotter steps 7–8):**
  
  7. **Build on the Change**
     - Analyze what went right and wrong.
     - Remain engaged.
  8. **Anchor the Changes in Organizational Culture**
     - Update instructions/policy.
     - Incorporate change into training.
     - Set new goals.

### Identify common resistance to change.

Resistance is most commonly encountered during the **Unfreezing** stage:

- **Distrust:** Members do not trust leadership's motives or the change itself.
- **Uncertainty:** Fear of the unknown and what the change will mean for them.
- **Self-Interest:** Negative self-interest — the perception that the change costs the member something personally.
- **Different Perception / No Felt Need to Change:** Members do not see a problem with the current state, so the change appears unnecessary.
- **Over-Determination:** Organizational structures, policies, and processes are built to reinforce stability, which inherently resists change.

### List the methods leaders use to reduce and manage resistance to change.

- **Follow the plan:** Enact the proposed vision and change plan deliberately rather than ad hoc.
- **Communicate:** Create awareness and understanding; ensure members know the "why" and "how." Communication is the primary tool against distrust and uncertainty.
- **Create urgency and a shared vision:** Give members a felt need to change and something to move toward.
- **Form a powerful coalition:** Build a group of influencers to carry the change and assist with policy changes.
- **Provide support:** Instill knowledge and develop ability so members are equipped to succeed under the new state.
- **Remove barriers to change:** Empower members by eliminating obstacles and updating policies that block the change.
- **Set short-term and long-term goals:** Use quick wins to reach milestones and reduce criticism; recognize strong performers.
- **Address concerns and resistance immediately:** Do not let resistance go unaddressed during the Changing stage.
- **Analyze and evaluate changes:** Monitor all change, evaluate whether it is achieving desired results, and determine whether more support or resources are needed.
- **Anchor the change:** Update instructions/policy, incorporate the change into training, and set new goals to prevent backsliding.

**Knowledge Check**

1. *What are the stages of Lewin's model?* — Unfreeze, Change, and Refreeze.
2. *What are some common forms of resistance?* — Distrust, uncertainty, negative self-interest, different perceptions, and over-determination.
3. *List some of the responsibilities of a leader in a change effort.* — Follow the plan, communicate, provide support, remove barriers to change, set short-term and long-term goals, analyze and evaluate changes.

## 11 – Inspiring Action

### Recognize the difference between mission and vision statements.

- **Mission Statement**
  - Focuses on the present
  - Defines the organization's purpose and what it does
  - Answers the "what" and "who"
  - Guides daily operations and decision-making
- **Vision Statement**
  - Focuses on the future
  - Describes the organization's long-term aspirations
  - Answers the "why" and "how"
  - Provides inspiration, hope, and a sense of direction

### Recall the purpose of using the SWOT method to identify priorities.

- **Stands For:** Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats.
- **Purpose:** A strategic planning tool to identify priorities and make decisions.
- **Function:**
  - Reveals positive forces to leverage and potential problems to address.
  - Assesses internal factors (Strengths, Weaknesses) and external factors (Opportunities, Threats).
  - Helps prioritize initiatives and allocate resources effectively.
  - Provides a foundation for creating a tangible action plan.

### Describe techniques for encouraging continued action towards a vision.

- **Articulate the Vision:** Communicate a clear, well-understood, and inspiring vision. Use storytelling to make it relatable and memorable.
- **Get Their Attention & Frame Meaning:**
  - Explain the "why" behind the vision to give it purpose.
  - Create a dialogue, not a monologue; invite questions and feedback.
- **Enable Action:**
  - Leaders must integrate the vision into the organization's daily activities.
  - Encourage two-way communication and welcome feedback for refinement.
- **Reinforce Action & Celebrate Success:**
  - Regularly discuss the vision in meetings and communications to keep it a priority.
  - Acknowledge progress and celebrate milestones to maintain motivation.

## 12 – Organizational Climate

### Contrast the difference between climate and culture.

- **Organizational Culture**
  - Long-term, ingrained ideologies, values, and norms.
  - Foundational beliefs and established traditions.
  - *Example:* ALWAYS WITH HONOR
- **Organizational Climate**
  - Short-term, adjustable attitudes and perceptions.
  - Current moods, feelings, and a product of the culture.
  - *Example:* How personnel currently and collectively feel about wearing those reflective belts within the dorms in PTU.

### Discuss strategies to build a positive organizational climate.

- **Safe and Secure Environment**
  - **Physical Safety:** Safeguard troops from harm, eliminate hazards, control risks through proper training and safety protocols.
  - **Emotional Safety:** Foster respect, openness, and trust; encourage calculated risks without fear of failure or harsh repercussions.
  - Uphold a "zero tolerance" policy for harassment and sexual assault.
- **High Standards**
  - Challenge personnel to reach new heights and conquer new challenges.
  - Clearly define standards by establishing and communicating expectations.
  - Connect individual expectations to the success of the overall mission.

### Describe how to positively change an organizational climate.

- **Address Your Unit**
  - Be transparent with survey results, like the DEOCS (Defense Equal Opportunity Climate Survey).
  - Discuss negative/positive trends and potential mission impact.
  - Offer follow-up services like one-on-one meetings or chaplain services.
- **Inspire Action**
  - Communicate the mission, vision, and priorities often and in various ways.
  - Empower subordinates, build trust, and foster collaboration.
  - Reinforce positive actions by showing appreciation and celebrating contributions.
- **Focus on the Little Things**
  - A leader's influence is constant and comes from every action.
  - *Examples:* Highlighting achievements, consistently providing feedback, enforcing policy, or offering simple words of encouragement.

### 1. Recognize how the US uses instruments of power to advance national interests.

**Instruments of national power** = the tools a nation uses to influence other nations, international organizations, or non-state actors. The core four are **DIME**.

| | Instrument | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D** | **Diplomatic** | The **principal** instrument for engaging other states and foreign groups to advance national values, interests, and objectives, and to solicit foreign support for military operations. Principal means of organizing coalitions and alliances. Power derives from type/stability of government, soundness of foreign policy, and international commitments. | Embassies/ambassadors, recognition, negotiations, treaties, policies, international forums |
| **I** | **Informational** | Determined by factors like population education and skill levels — the more literate a nation, the more effectively it can wield informational power. Includes cultural and student exchanges, international conferences, and covert deception/misinformation programs. | Military information, public diplomacy, communications resources, international forums, spokespersons, timing/media/venue of announcements |
| **M** | **Military** | **Fundamentally coercive in nature.** Generates effects through the application (or threat) of force to compel an adversary to take or discontinue an action. Also useful in non-conflict situations such as foreign relief. Varies in purpose, scale, risk, and combat intensity across the **Range of Military Operations (ROMO)**. | Military operations, engagement/security cooperation/deterrence, show of force, military technology, size and composition of force |
| **E** | **Economic** | A strong economy with free access to global markets is a fundamental engine of general welfare and enabler of a strong national defense. Derived from population, natural resources, and monetary stability. Treasury works with USG agencies, foreign governments, and international financial institutions. | Trade policies, fiscal and monetary policies, embargoes, tariffs, assistance, sanctions |

**Interaction of the instruments**

- The instruments are **interrelated and work best when orchestrated together**; they should rarely, if ever, be used in isolation.
- The **President** has ultimate responsibility for national security policy and therefore for developing, coordinating, and employing the IOPs.
- Departments and agencies interact to build mutual understanding of capabilities, limitations, and consequences of military and civilian actions, and to identify how military and nonmilitary capabilities **complement** each other.
- The **National Security Strategy (NSS)** describes how the President plans to use the IOPs to achieve national objectives.

> **Joint expansion (Purple Book):** the joint community uses **DIME-FIL** — adding **Financial**, **Intelligence**, and **Law Enforcement** — under a *whole of government* approach.

### 2. Recall the process for formulating US military strategy from national objectives.

**Lykke's Model — Ends, Ways, Means** *(Col Arthur F. Lykke Jr.; became the dominant US Army strategy model in the 1980s)*

| Component | Question | Definition | Example
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ends** | **WHAT** is to be accomplished | The **objectives** that create or contribute to the desired end state and ultimately serve national interests. Expressed with **verbs**. *(deter war, promote regional stability, denuclearize the peninsula)* | National Security Strategy |
| **Ways** | **HOW** the ends are accomplished | The **strategic concepts and courses of action**. Must be explicit enough to give planning guidance to those who implement and resource it. Often contain a verb, but state "how," not "what." *(containment, forward defense, mutually assured destruction, forward presence)* | National Defense Strategy |
| **Means** | **WITH WHAT** | The **specific resources**. **Tangible:** forces, people, equipment, money, facilities. **Intangible:** will, courage, intellect. | National Military Strategy |

- **Test for a "way":** ask *"in order to do what?"* — the answer reveals the real objective (the End).
- **Worked example:** End = "defend the United States" (what) → Way = "develop, build, or establish a larger force" (how) → Means = "national manpower reserves, money, and training facilities."
- **The linkage:** national objectives are the **Ends**; the four instruments of power are the **Means**. These are **continuously evaluated and matched**.
- Strategy also carries **risk** — the gap that appears when ends, ways, and means are out of balance.

**Where the NSS fits**

- The NSS is the President's articulation of national interests and how the IOPs will be used to advance them.
- Subordinate departments and agencies translate and refine the NSS into their own strategies (e.g., **NDS**, then **NMS**) — cascading national objectives down into military strategy.

## 14 – What is War

> **Cognitive Samples of Behavior**
> 1. Identify the three enduring truths that describe the fundamental nature of war.
> 2. Identify the two basic themes of war.
> 3. List the three factors that dominate war.
> 4. Differentiate between the traditional and irregular forms of warfare.

### Framing: Clausewitz and the definition of war

**Carl von Clausewitz** (1780–1831) — Prussian staff officer who fought Napoleon, later President of the Prussian War Academy. Author of the eight-volume ***Vom Kriege (On War)***.

- War, in its simplest form, is **"a duel on an extensive scale."**
- **Definition: "War is an act of violence to compel our opponent to do our will."**
- This reveals the **two basic and inseparable aspects** of war:
  - **Policy** — the "will" a nation-state seeks to impose; answers **WHY** the war is fought.
  - **Violence** — the raw, physical essence; the "act" itself. The application of force and destruction of the enemy's armed forces.

### 2. Identify the two basic themes of war. *(Nature and Character)*

| | **Nature of War** | **Character of War** |
|---|---|---|
| **Changes?** | **Unchanging and permanent** | **In constant flux** |
| **What it is** | War's essential, unalterable purpose: to use violence, or the threat of it, to achieve a political objective | **How** warfare is conducted — its appearance and form |
| **Why** | War is a profoundly **human** activity, tied to unchanging human nature (passion, chance, political aim) | Reflects the political, economic, military, and technological dynamics of a given society |
| **Examples** | Violence, human fallibility, and politics — as relevant today as in the age of empires | Tanks and airplanes in WWI · atomic weapons in WWII · widespread drone use in the Russia–Ukraine war |

### 1. Identify the three enduring truths.

**Enduring Truth #1 — War is an instrument of policy.**

- War is an act of violence, but **it is not an end**. War between nations always starts from a political condition and political motive; therefore war is a **political act**.
- Once begun, war does not set aside policy and follow its own violent path. **The original political motive must be the first and highest consideration** in the conduct of war.
- *"War is not merely a political act, but also a real political instrument, a continuation of political commerce, a carrying out of the same by other means."* The **political view is the object; war is the means.**
- **Example:** the **First Gulf War (1991)** — the political object was clear and limited (liberate Kuwait). Once achieved by military force, the war was halted: a near-perfect subordination of military means to the political goal.

**The three levels of war** *(the mechanism that converts political objectives into military action — it is **not the weapon used, but the desired effect**, that determines the level):*

| Level | Question | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strategic** | **WHY** and **WITH WHAT** | National security objectives are set; all IOPs (DIME) are made available. Effects impair the adversary's overall ability to carry on the war and neutralize fundamental **centers of gravity**. | Air strike to remove an enemy regime and destroy national will — e.g., ending Saddam Hussein's rule in OIF |
| **Operational** | **WHAT** to affect, with **WHAT** COAs, in **WHAT** order, for **WHAT** duration | The realm of **campaigns and major operations** that sequence tactical actions to produce effects exceeding the sum of their parts. | B-52 strikes on an enemy **integrated air defense system (IADS)** — shapes the battlespace and enables future tactical action |
| **Tactical** | **HOW** | Individual **battles and engagements**. Direct and tangible violence; the "duel" in its most literal sense. Focused on defeating an enemy force at a specific time and place. | A-10 conducting **close air support** for friendly forces in contact |

**Enduring Truth #2 — War is a complex and chaotic human endeavor.**

- War appears simple without personal experience, but the simplest actions become difficult. These difficulties accumulate and produce **friction** — what **distinguishes real war from war on paper**.
- The military machine is composed entirely of individuals, each with their own frailties. **Danger + human exertion = friction.**

**Enduring Truth #3 — War is a clash of opposing wills.**

- War is not waged against an inanimate mass but against a **living, calculating, and adaptive opponent**.
- It is always the **shock of two hostile bodies in collision**. *As long as the enemy is not defeated, I must recognize that he may defeat me.*
- This dynamic is the **engine that drives conflict forward**.

### 3. List the three factors that dominate war. *(Fog, Friction, Chance)*

| Factor | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fog** | The **imperfect knowledge** within warfare — how difficult it is to see and understand what is going on once the battle starts. | **Battle of the Bulge (1944)** — Allied high command, in a fog of overconfidence and faulty intelligence, was completely surprised by the German offensive through the Ardennes. |
| **Friction** | The **accumulation of thousands of little things** that force plans to go awry. | **Operation BARBAROSSA (1941)** — the plan relied on speed, but mud, logistical breakdowns over vast distances, and minor delays compounding into major ones halted the advance before Moscow. |
| **Chance** | **Dumb luck and fortune**, which play an enormous role in war. | **Operation VALKYRIE (1944)** — the briefcase bomb's blast was deflected by a heavy oak table leg, sparing Hitler. Also the **USS Indianapolis** — the Japanese commander testified it was a lucky shot on one last look through the periscope. |

### 4. Differentiate between traditional and irregular warfare.

*(These are **not mutually exclusive**, but differ significantly in strategy and conduct.)*

| | **Traditional Warfare** | **Irregular Warfare** |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | A violent struggle for **domination between nation-states** | A violent struggle among **state and non-state actors** for **legitimacy and influence over relevant populations** |
| **Actors** | Nation-states | State and non-state actors |
| **What's contested** | Domination | Legitimacy and influence over populations |
| **Approach** | Force-on-force operations using **conventional capabilities** | **Indirect and asymmetric** approaches |
| **Objective** | Disarm the enemy — destroy his military, conquer his country, subdue his will | Raise the **price of success** until the enemy's political object is no longer equal in value; **weary out** the enemy through gradual exhaustion of physical powers and will |
| **Example** | **Napoleonic Wars** — at **Austerlitz (1805)** Napoleon achieved decisive destruction of the Austro-Russian forces in a single day, leading Austria to sue for peace. The "bloody solution of the crisis." | **Soviet-Afghan War (1979–1989)** — the Mujahideen could not defeat the Soviets conventionally, so they waged a protracted guerrilla campaign draining Soviet resources and manpower until the price of occupation became too high. |

### Final thoughts — Clausewitz's "Wonderful Trinity"

War is a whole composed of three competing tendencies, each a different "lawgiver":

1. **Primordial violence, hatred, and blind instinct** → the **passion of the PEOPLE**
2. **The play of chance and probability** → creative action; the courage and talent of the **COMMANDER and MILITARY**
3. **Subordination as a political instrument** → the pure reason of the **GOVERNMENT**

> The great problem for any student of war is to **keep these tendencies in balance**. War is not a game, but a grave means to a serious end. Hold a complete, multifaceted vision: war as a **political act**, a **human endeavor**, and a **clash of wills**.

## 16 – Military Planning Fundamentals

> **Cognitive Measurable Samples of Behavior**
> 1. Explain why the military uses a formal planning process.
> 2. Summarize the seven steps of the Air Force Planning Process.
> 3. Describe the attributes of a quality course of action (COA).
> 4. Summarize the different methods used to evaluate COAs.
> 5. Recall the purpose of wargaming.

### 1. Explain why the military uses a formal planning process.

**The core argument:** a structured framework **cannot guarantee success**, but it **minimizes risk and maximizes the chances of success**, and it forces a realistic assessment of challenges and risks.

**What a formal process does for you:**

- Forces you to **explore alternatives, challenge assumptions, consider different perspectives, and justify your reasoning**.
- Cultivates **adaptability and creative problem-solving** through iterative analysis, experimentation, communication, and refinement.
- Fosters **disciplined initiative** and supports **decentralized execution** by distributing the commander's intent.
- Gives the commander the ability to make **timely decisions in dynamic and complex environments**.
- Valuable in any role, regardless of career field or strategic involvement.

**The case for change — Operation EAGLE CLAW (24 April 1980)**

The failed rescue of 52 American hostages in Tehran. Two helicopters aborted with mechanical problems and a third suffered hydraulic failure, leaving **five operational helicopters against a six-helicopter minimum**. Col Charles Beckwith aborted. During withdrawal from **Desert One**, a helicopter repositioning for refueling collided with a parked C-130 — **eight American servicemen killed** (five Airmen, three Marines). The hostages remained captive another 264 days.

**Six contributing planning failures — know these:**

1. **Overly complex plan** — intricate details, multiple services, highly coordinated sequence over vast distances and unfamiliar terrain → cascading failures.
2. **Insufficient rehearsals** — units had limited opportunity to train together in conditions replicating the Iranian desert.
3. **Inadequate weather contingency planning** — planners knew of the risk, but plans were insufficient; unexpected sandstorms caused delays and malfunctions.
4. **Lack of redundancy** — loss of a single helicopter had a cascading effect on a plan dependent on an exact aircraft count.
5. **Interservice coordination breakdowns** — communication and coordination failures between sister services caused delays and confusion.
6. **Over-reliance on advanced technology** — equipment proved unreliable in the harsh desert environment.

> **Contrast case — Operation DESERT STORM (1991).** Structured planning produced a clear primary objective (swiftly and decisively liberate Kuwait) with clear metrics for success; emphasis on intelligence and targeting identified key Iraqi weaknesses. The process also **defined the limits of intervention** — no regime change — which **prevented mission creep** and maintained international support.

### 2. Summarize the seven steps of the Air Force Planning Process.

*(AFDP 5-0; mirrors the Joint Planning Process in JP 5-0. Grouped into three bands: **Strategic Guidance** → **Developing the Concept** → **Developing the Plan**.)*

| # | Step | Key Activity | Deliverables |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Planning Initiation** | Kicks off the process. Craft a clear, concise **problem statement** using factual data (the 5Ws + How). Focus on defining the **problem**, not proposing solutions. Must align with **commander's intent** and be realistic in scope. | Well-defined problem statement; a planning team |
| 2 | **Mission Analysis** | *Arguably the most critical step.* Refine the **mission statement** with a clear vision of the **end state**, analyzed in the context of the **operational environment (OE)**. Define **constraints** (what you cannot do) and **restraints** (your limitations); understand adversary capabilities, intentions, and vulnerabilities. | Brief containing problem statement, mission statement, end state; OE understanding; directed and implied tasks; limitations and assumptions; potential risks |
| 3 | **COA Development** | Generate **multiple potential approaches**. Akin to brainstorming — each COA is a potential path to the end state while minimizing risk. Provide a sequencing of major actions and desired effects; craft a **COA statement** answering the 5Ws and how. | COA briefing |
| 4 | **COA Analysis and Wargaming** | Rigorously evaluate each COA for strengths, weaknesses, risks, and consequences. Consider time, resources, terrain, weather, and enemy capabilities. Uncover "what-if" scenarios and unintended consequences. | Most promising option with clear justification |
| 5 | **COA Comparison** | Systematically **rate** each COA on advantages, disadvantages, and risks found in wargaming, using rating criteria. | Recommended COA |
| 6 | **COA Approval** | Deliver a **decision brief** highlighting differences between COAs with an overall recommendation. **The commander decides.** | Commander-approved COA |
| 7 | **Plan or Order Development** | Develop the detailed **OPLAN** (long-range planning) or **OPORD** (immediate execution) from the approved COA. | Finalized OPLAN/OPORD; mission rehearsal plan |

**The framework is linear but the process is iterative.** Each step builds on the previous one, and arrows lead back from Step 7 to Step 2 (or even Step 1) — planning is often revisited and refined.

**At Step 6, the commander has six options:**
1. Concur with the recommended COA
2. Select a different COA
3. Modify a COA
4. Combine aspects of multiple COAs
5. Direct development of different COAs
6. Request additional information

### 3. Describe the attributes of a quality COA.

Each COA must be **adequate, feasible, acceptable, distinguishable, and complete**.

> **Mnemonic: A-F-A-D-C** — *"**A** **F**ully **A**dequate **D**ecision **C**hecklist."*

| Attribute | Core Question | Supporting Questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Suitable** | Will this COA **achieve the objective**? | Does it align with the overall mission and strategic goals? Is it the best way to achieve the objective? |
| **Feasible** | Is it **executable within available time and space**? | Do we have enough resources? The necessary skills and expertise? Any legal or regulatory constraints? |
| **Acceptable** | Is the **potential benefit worth the risk**? | What are the potential negative consequences? Any ethical considerations? |
| **Distinguishable** | How is this COA **different** from the others? | Does it offer a unique approach? What are its advantages/disadvantages relative to the others? |
| **Complete** | Does it incorporate **all critical tasks** and the full scope of the operation? | Does it define roles and responsibilities? Include a realistic timeline? Identify risks and mitigation strategies? |

**Elements of a solid COA briefing:**
- Purpose, situation overview, mission statement, assumptions, and constraints/limitations
- Presentation of the COAs — summary of major tasks, timeline, and end state
- Overview of resource support and a **risk assessment with mitigation strategies**
- **Justification** for why a particular COA is best, including advantages and disadvantages of each

### 4. Summarize the different methods used to evaluate COAs.

| Method | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Wargaming** *(Step 4)* | Action–reaction–counteraction against a thinking adversary; surfaces "what-if" scenarios and unintended consequences before commitment. |
| **Evaluation matrix** | The tool of choice for many. Scores each COA against a set of **rating criteria**. |
| **Weighted criteria** | Criteria are assigned relative importance (e.g., 10 = most important, 1 = least) before scoring, so the comparison reflects what actually matters to the commander. |
| **Pairwise / head-to-head comparison** | Analyze COAs two at a time, identifying which is superior in **each category and why**. Count the number of times each COA is deemed superior to determine relative ranking. |
| **Sensitivity analysis** | Examine how the **ranking would change** if certain **assumptions or conditions** changed. |
| **Trade-off identification** | Identify key trade-offs in choosing one COA over another (e.g., higher risk for potentially greater reward). |

> Always present the recommendation against the **commander's priorities and risk tolerance**.
>
> *Sample criteria from the DESERT SHIELD exercise:* Speed of Liberation · Coalition Casualties · Iraqi Casualties · Political Acceptability · Logistical Feasibility · Long-Term Regional Stability.

### 5. Recall the purpose of wargaming.

Wargames provide a **structured environment** to:

- **Simplify complex problems**
- **Challenge assumptions**
- **Enable the team to learn** from the experience
- **Visualize the flow of operations**
- Simulate the **human decision-making processes inherent in war**, including the **"fog and friction"** of real-world scenarios
- Let the team **experience the consequences of their actions against a thinking adversary**

**Structure:** a table-top exercise typically pits a **Blue Team** (friendly forces) executing the COAs against a **Red Team** (adversary) reacting and countering — *"a chess game of action, reaction, and counteraction."*

## 18 – Military Justice

> **Cognitive Measurable Samples of Behavior**
> 1. Identify the functions of the Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ) and Manual for Courts-Martial (MCM).
> 2. Recognize the need for a military justice system.
> 3. List service members' rights within the military justice system.
> 4. Identify the methods and associated tools available within the UCMJ to maintain discipline.
> 5. Recall the functions of the Staff Judge Advocate, Area Defense Counsel, and the Victims' Counsel.

### 1. Identify the functions of the UCMJ and MCM.

- **Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ)**
  - Federal law that is the basis of the military justice system.
  - Modified and consolidated the Articles of War, Articles for the Government of the Navy, and Disciplinary Laws of the Coast Guard.
  - Contains the Punitive Articles (Articles 77–134).
  - Applies to all members of the armed forces worldwide.
- **Manual for Courts-Martial (MCM)**
  - Implemented by Executive Order from the President.
  - Has the full force and effect of law.
  - Provides detailed rules and procedures for implementing the UCMJ.
  - Contains the full texts of the UCMJ and the US Constitution, rules of evidence, and guides for writing charges.

### 2. Recognize the need for a military justice system.

- **Crimes Unique to the Military**
  - Addresses offenses that have no equivalent in civilian life, such as AWOL (Article 86), Disrespect Toward a Superior (Article 89), and Failure to Obey a Lawful Order (Article 92).
- **Discipline**
  - The system is a driving force behind military discipline.
  - Critical to maintaining good order, efficiency, and effectiveness for mission success.
- **Worldwide Operations**
  - Provides a transportable legal system that applies to service members wherever they are stationed or deployed globally, as the US Constitution doesn't extend US judicial power to foreign soil.

### 3. List service members' rights within the military justice system.

- **Right Against Compulsory Self-Incrimination (Article 31)**
  - The military counterpart to the Fifth Amendment / *Miranda* warnings for civilians.
  - Must be informed of the nature of the suspected offense.
  - Right to remain silent and stop questioning at any time.
  - Any statements made can be used against them in a court-martial.
  - Statements obtained **without** an Article 31 advisement — or through coercion or unlawful influence — are inadmissible.
- **Right to Counsel**
  - Free military counsel is provided to all accused before **summary, special, and general** courts-martial, and to members offered **Article 15** punishment.
  - Provided by the **Area Defense Counsel (ADC)**; a civilian counsel may be hired at the member's own expense.
- **Against Unreasonable Search & Seizure**
  - Commanders can authorize searches in areas under their authority; the **SJA should be consulted** before a search or seizure in most cases.
  - Warrants are required for searches of personal property outside a commander's authority.
  - Health and welfare inspections are permissible but **cannot be a pretext** for a criminal search.
  - Evidence seized in an illegal search or inspection **cannot be used** for an Article 15 or court-martial.

### 4. Identify the methods and associated tools available within the UCMJ to maintain discipline.

**Preventive Discipline (first line)**

- Action taken to encourage members to follow standards and prevent infractions before they occur.
- Objective is to encourage **self-discipline** rather than have discipline imposed by superiors.
- Communicates standards, explains the *reasoning* behind them, and encourages member participation in setting them.
- State standards positively ("Safety first!") rather than negatively ("Don't be careless!").

**When members do not comply, there are three broad options:**

**a. Administrative Tools** *(educational and corrective, not retaliatory)*

- Usually initiated by the immediate supervisor; some require commander action/approval.
- **Three objectives of administrative action:**
  1. To reform the offender
  2. To deter others from similar actions
  3. To maintain consistent, effective group standards
- Tools:
  - **Letter of Counseling (LOC):** Documents verbal counseling for minor infractions.
  - **Letter of Admonishment (LOA):** More severe than a LOC for more serious infractions.
  - **Letter of Reprimand (LOR):** Official censure; more severe than LOC or LOA.
  - **Unfavorable Information File (UIF):** Official record of unfavorable information about conduct and performance. Some items (e.g., Article 15s, court-martial convictions, officer LORs) are **mandatory** filings.
  - **Control Roster:** Establishes a 6-month observation/rehabilitation period. **Not a punishment**, but affects eligibility for reenlistment, promotion, and PCS. See DAFI 36-2907.
  - **Officer Selection Record (OSR):** Documents used by promotion boards to evaluate officer promotion.

**b. Nonjudicial Punishment (NJP)**

- Authorized by **Article 15, UCMJ** — punishment *not* imposed by a court of justice.
- For **minor offenses**; more serious than an LOC/LOA/LOR but **not a criminal conviction**.
- Purpose: maintain morale, discipline, and efficiency; correct or rehabilitate the offender.
- May be recommended by other officers or NCOs, but **imposed by the commander**.
- **Member's rights:** may **demand trial by court-martial in lieu of** accepting the Article 15, and may **consult the ADC** before deciding.

**c. Judicial Punishment (Courts-Martial)**

| Type | Tries | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Summary (SCM)** | Minor offenses, **enlisted only** | Normally one officer; accused may **refuse** and demand a special court-martial |
| **Special (SPCM)** | Intermediate offenses | DAF policy provides a military judge on every SPCM |
| **General (GCM)** | Most serious offenses | Possibility of maximum punishment |

### 5. Recall the functions of the SJA, ADC, and VC.

- **Staff Judge Advocate (SJA)**
  - The commander's legal staff; represents the Air Force and Space Force in all legal matters.
  - Advises commanders on military justice, investigations, searches/seizures, and disciplinary actions.
  - Manages the installation legal office, covering military justice, claims, and civil law.
- **Area Defense Counsel (ADC)**
  - Represents Air Force and Space Force personnel accused of wrongdoing, **all ranks**.
  - Operates through a **separate chain of command** to ensure independence.
  - Provides advice and representation for criminal investigations, LORs, Article 15s, administrative actions, and courts-martial.
- **Victims' Counsel (VC)**
  - Advises and represents victims of alleged sexual assault and domestic violence.
  - Assists victims with their rights and can represent them in certain matters during courts-martial.
  - Operates through a **separate chain of command** from the SJA.
